The question "how much does a small business website cost?" has a thousand answers on the internet and almost none of them are useful. They give you a range from $500 to $50,000, say "it depends," and leave you exactly where you started.

Here's the short version: a professionally built small business website costs **$4,000–$15,000 in 2026**, with ongoing costs of $1,200–$4,000 per year. Everything below that range is DIY. Everything above it is custom. The rest of this guide explains exactly what you get at each level — and why the cheapest option almost always costs more in the end.

<div id="the-short-answer"></div>

## The short answer: small business website cost at a glance

| Tier | Who it's for | Upfront cost | Annual ongoing | 3-year TCO |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| DIY builder | Pre-revenue, testing an idea | $0–$500 | $240–$600 | $720–$2,300 |
| Freelance designer | Established service business | $1,500–$8,000 | $600–$2,400 | $3,300–$15,200 |
| Boutique agency | Website is primary lead channel | $6,000–$20,000 | $1,200–$4,000 | $9,600–$32,000 |
| Full-service agency | Multi-location, complex builds | $20,000–$60,000 | $2,400–$10,000 | $27,200–$90,000 |
| Custom build | Enterprise-level complexity | $40,000–$100,000+ | $5,000–$20,000 | $55,000–$160,000+ |

**Total cost of ownership (TCO)** is the number that actually matters — the full three-year spend including the initial build, hosting, maintenance, and any rebuilds. The upfront number is the one everyone quotes. The TCO is the one you'll actually pay.

The gap between upfront cost and TCO is where most small businesses get surprised. A $2,000 freelance site with a $150/month maintenance plan costs you $7,400 over three years. That's a different conversation than "$2,000."

## Tier 1: DIY website builders — $0–$500 upfront, $240–$600/year

DIY builders are the right choice for a narrow slice of businesses: pre-revenue startups testing a concept, pop-up businesses that may not exist in two years, or solo operators who genuinely enjoy managing a website.

For everyone else, the economics don't work as well as they appear.

### What it actually costs

The subscription is just the start. A Squarespace Business plan runs $23/month; Wix Core is $17/month; Shopify Basic is $39/month for ecommerce. Add a domain ($10–$20/year), any premium templates ($50–$150), and third-party apps for booking, forms, or email marketing ($10–$50/month each), and you're quickly at $600–$1,200/year.

That's before you account for your time.

### The time trap

Building a DIY site from scratch takes most non-technical business owners 40–80 hours the first time. At a $50/hour opportunity cost — a conservative figure for most business owners — that's $2,000–$4,000 in labour for a site that would have cost $3,500 with a decent freelancer. And unlike a freelancer, the DIY version usually looks like it took 40 hours.

Ongoing updates take another 3–5 hours per month for someone who doesn't know what they're doing.

### When to move on from DIY

The moment your website is doing real business work — booking calls, generating leads, representing you in competitive searches — you've outgrown the DIY tier. Specific triggers: you're turning away work, you're raising your prices, you're hiring, or a competitor just launched a site that makes yours look embarrassing.

## Tier 2: Freelance web designer — $1,500–$8,000

This is the most underrated tier in the market — and almost all the content ranking about it is written by agencies, which explains why it's framed as risky.

For an established service business investing $3,500–$6,000 with a vetted freelancer, you typically get better attention, faster communication, and output comparable to a $12,000 agency project. You're not paying for account management overhead, weekly status calls, or a business development team's salaries.

### What drives the price range

A $1,500 freelance site is a template with your content dropped in. That's a legitimate product, but be clear-eyed about what it is.

A $5,000–$8,000 freelance project typically includes custom design (not a template), 5–10 pages, basic SEO setup, and a CMS so you can update content yourself. Whether copywriting is included depends on the freelancer — always ask.

The variables that push a quote higher: page count above ten, custom functionality (booking systems, calculators, membership areas), e-commerce integration, and whether the freelancer writes copy or you provide it.

### Three questions to ask before hiring a freelancer

**"Can I see three sites you've built in the past 12 months, and will you introduce me to those clients?"** A confident freelancer says yes immediately. Hesitation is a flag.

**"Do you build on a staging environment before the site goes live?"** If they don't know what this means, keep looking. A staging environment is a private copy of the site where changes are tested before they affect your live site.

**"What happens if I need changes six months after launch?"** This tells you whether they have a maintenance plan, an hourly rate, or whether they disappear. All three are valid — you just need to know which one.

### Red flags in a freelance quote

- No written contract or scope document
- "Free hosting" included (they're reselling hosting at a markup, often on overcrowded shared servers)
- Payment required in full upfront
- No mention of a staging or testing phase
- Portfolio sites that all look identical (template shops)

## Tier 3: Boutique agency — $6,000–$20,000

A boutique agency is a studio of two to ten people. You get a team — usually a strategist, designer, and developer — without the overhead of a large agency. This is the tier where the strategy layer starts to appear.

**Who actually needs a boutique agency:** businesses where the website is the primary lead channel, where ranking in competitive local search matters, or where you need branded copywriting alongside the design. Law firms, medical practices, service businesses in competitive markets, and any business running paid advertising that lands on the website.

### What you're actually buying

A competent boutique agency isn't selling you a website. It's selling you a documented process: discovery and strategy first, wireframes before design, design before development, testing before launch. That process is what makes the result predictable.

What you pay for between $6,000 and $20,000: a discovery phase (interviews, competitor review, user research), wireframes, custom design, copywriting (sometimes), SEO structure built in from the start, and typically 30–90 days of post-launch support.

### Price signals: quality vs. padding

A $6,000 boutique agency quote and a $20,000 boutique agency quote are not the same product. The honest difference is usually page count, complexity of functionality, and whether copywriting is included.

Watch for padding: project management fees listed separately, "brand guidelines" that turn out to be a PDF of your fonts and colours, and SEO packages that are just keyword research. Ask what each line item actually produces.

A quality proposal always includes a discovery phase, a revision schedule (not "unlimited revisions"), and a defined timeline.

## Tier 4: Full-service agencies and custom builds — $20,000–$100,000+

Most small businesses that end up spending $30,000+ on a website didn't need to. They got sold something bigger than their actual requirements, usually because they didn't know what questions to ask.

The businesses that genuinely need this tier: multi-location enterprises where each location needs its own SEO presence, complex e-commerce stores with hundreds of SKUs and custom pricing logic, regulated industries (financial services, healthcare) with compliance requirements baked into the architecture, or businesses building a custom CMS alongside the site.

Custom build means custom CMS architecture, API integrations with back-office systems, and often a brand identity system delivered alongside. It's a category where DesigXner typically refers clients out — these projects are a different discipline from what most web design agencies do well.

**The tell:** a good agency at this tier will push back if your requirements don't justify the price. If they're happy to take $40,000 for a five-page service site, keep shopping.

## The real cost of a cheap website

There's a pattern in how cheap websites actually cost money. It goes like this:

A service business launches a $500–$1,500 website. It looks fine. Six months later, it's loading slowly because it's on cheap shared hosting. Google starts to penalise it for poor Core Web Vitals — the performance signals Google uses to rank pages. It was never built with local SEO in mind, so it's invisible in competitive searches. The owner makes a few updates and breaks something; fixing it costs $200.

Eighteen months in, the site is generating almost nothing. The owner hires a freelancer to rebuild it — $3,500. The migration from the old host to the new one hits a snag: $400. The content was written for the old site and needs rewriting: $1,200. Leads missed over 18 months of underperformance: impossible to calculate precisely, but for a service business that converts one in four website inquiries at $2,000 per client, even two fewer leads per month is $72,000 in potential revenue.

> Industry observers consistently note that underdeveloped sites require another redesign within two years or less — a pattern we see repeatedly with clients who come to us after a budget build. No large-scale published study has quantified the exact rebuild rate, which is why DesigXner is running an original survey of 30 SMBs to document what they paid, how long each build lasted, and what the total cost of the cycle actually came to. Results will be published in the [State of SMB Web Design 2026 report](/insights/process/state-of-smb-web-design-2026/).

The cost of cheap isn't about design quality. It's about rebuild cycles. If a website will need replacing within 24 months, the money you saved upfront is an illusion. The only number that matters is the total cost of ownership over three years — and cheap websites almost always lose that comparison.

A second, subtler cost: credibility. Buyers research before they contact anyone. A website that looks underdeveloped signals to a potential client that the business is either new or not invested in its own presentation. For service businesses in competitive markets, this is not a theoretical risk — it's the reason a prospect clicks back and calls your competitor instead.

The rule: if your website is the primary thing a prospect sees before they decide whether to contact you, it should represent the same level of professionalism as your best work.

## Hidden costs every small business misses

The upfront build cost is the most visible number. These are the ones that don't appear in the proposal.

**Content creation.** Somebody has to write the copy for every page. If you do it yourself, plan for 20–60 hours of work. If you hire a copywriter, budget $500–$3,000 depending on how many pages and how much research is involved. This is consistently the number-one hidden cost across the industry — and the number-one reason website projects stall.

**Photography.** Stock photos make service businesses look generic. A professional half-day shoot costs $300–$1,500 and produces assets that last for years. It's one of the highest-ROI investments a service business can make.

**Domain name.** Standard .com domains run $10–$20/year. If your preferred domain is taken, you're either paying a premium ($500–$50,000+ for desirable names) or settling for a second-choice domain. Buy your domain before you start the project.

**Hosting.** Shared hosting costs $50–$150/year and is adequate for low-traffic brochure sites. Managed WordPress hosting — which handles security updates, backups, and performance optimisation — runs $100–$500/year and is worth it for any site generating serious business. Some agencies host your site for you at $20–$80/month; this is convenient but creates dependency.

**SSL certificate.** SSL (the padlock in the browser) is now free via Let's Encrypt. If anyone quotes you for SSL in 2026, ask why.

**Maintenance and updates.** Professional maintenance plans run $40–$300/month and cover security monitoring, plugin updates, uptime monitoring, and small content changes. If you skip a maintenance plan and manage WordPress yourself, budget 2–4 hours per month for updates and the occasional broken plugin.

**SEO.** A website built without SEO structure is not going to rank for anything competitive. If your build quote doesn't mention keyword research, page titles, meta descriptions, schema markup, or site speed, these aren't included. Budget $500–$2,000 for basic SEO setup, or factor it into your agency brief as a requirement.

**Payment processing.** If you're taking payments online, Stripe and Square charge 2.9% plus $0.30 per transaction. On $10,000/month in online revenue, that's $320/month — nearly $4,000/year — that most business owners don't model in advance.

## Is a website worth it? The ROI math

Stop thinking about your website as an expense. Think about it as a cost-per-lead machine.

Here's how to run the numbers:

**Example A:** You invest $8,000 in a boutique agency site. In the first year, it generates 12 qualified leads per month. Your total annual website cost (build amortised + hosting + maintenance) is roughly $10,400. That's **$72 per lead**. In year two, the build cost is paid; total spend drops to $2,400. That's **$17 per lead**.

**Example B:** You invest $500 in a DIY site. It generates 2 leads per month. Total annual cost is $1,200. That's **$50 per lead** — and there's no way to grow it without rebuilding it.

The DIY option looks cheaper per lead in year one. It isn't in year two. And the ceiling on a DIY site is two leads per month.

The ROI data supports investing properly. Krishaweb's 2026 benchmark data — aggregated from multiple agency datasets and client engagements — shows UX and CRO-focused redesigns producing an average **+41% conversion lift within three to six months**. Strategy-led redesigns (where discovery and positioning happen before any design begins) average a **+68% conversion lift within nine months**, with B2B businesses reporting 12-month ROI in the 287–342% range once lead volume and close rates are factored in.

Those numbers won't apply to every business. But the framework holds: **the metric to track is cost per inbound lead**, calculated as total annual website spend divided by qualified leads generated. If you don't know this number, you're flying blind on the single most controllable variable in your marketing.

## How to get a fair quote — and not overpay

Get three quotes. Not one, not two. Three — because you need a baseline, a comparison, and a sanity check.

But don't compare quotes on price. Compare them on scope. The cheapest quote is almost always the most incomplete one.

### Ask for an itemised breakdown

Any serious agency or freelancer will give you a line-item breakdown: design, copywriting, development, SEO setup, hosting, maintenance, and any third-party integrations listed separately. A lump-sum quote is a quote designed to hide what's not included.

### The one question that changes every conversation

Ask: **"What does this quote not include?"**

This forces the vendor to name the extras upfront. You'll typically hear: copywriting, photography, content migration, SEO, and ongoing support. Now you can compare apples to apples — or add those services to the scope before you sign anything.

### Red flags in any proposal

- **"Unlimited revisions"** — this phrase means there's no defined project scope and no project management. Every unlimited-revision project ends in scope creep and a strained relationship.
- No timeline or defined milestones
- No mention of a staging or testing environment
- No discovery or strategy phase (they're designing before they understand your business)
- Payment required in full before any work starts

### What a good proposal looks like

A quality proposal includes a discovery phase (1–2 weeks of research before any design begins), wireframes approved before visual design starts, a defined revision schedule (typically two rounds), a staging environment for testing, and a post-launch support period. If those elements are missing, the project will be harder to manage and less likely to produce what you expected.

## Frequently asked questions

### How much does a small business website cost in 2026?

A DIY builder (Wix, Squarespace) runs $240–$600 per year. A freelance designer charges $1,500–$8,000 as a one-time project fee. A boutique agency typically charges $6,000–$20,000. Custom builds start at $20,000. Most established small businesses investing in a professional site land between $4,000 and $12,000 all-in for the first year.

### What are the ongoing costs of a small business website?

Plan for $1,100–$5,000 per year in ongoing costs: hosting ($50–$500/year), maintenance ($500–$3,600/year for professional plans), domain renewal ($10–$20/year), and any paid plugins or tools. Content updates and SEO are additional if outsourced. The ongoing costs are what most quotes leave out — always ask for a 12-month total, not just the build price.

### Is a cheap website worth it for a small business?

Only if your business is pre-revenue or genuinely experimental. For an established business, a cheap website typically triggers a rebuild within 18–24 months. When you account for the rebuild cost, the missed leads during that period, and the time spent managing something that never quite works properly, the cheap option usually costs more over three years than doing it right the first time.

### How much should I budget for a small business website?

If you're a service business generating more than $100k/year in revenue, budget at least $3,500–$6,000 for a freelance build or $8,000–$15,000 for an agency build. If the website is your primary lead channel, treat it as a $10,000–$20,000 investment amortised over three years — roughly $280–$550/month — and measure it against your cost per lead.

### What's not included in most website quotes?

Copywriting, photography, SEO setup, hosting, ongoing maintenance, and any third-party integrations (booking tools, payment processors, CRM connectors) are routinely excluded from headline quotes. Always ask for an itemised breakdown and specifically ask what the quote does not include. You'll almost always find $2,000–$6,000 in extras that weren't mentioned upfront.
